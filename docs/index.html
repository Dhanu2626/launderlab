An answer key for anti-money-laundering detection

Every bank runs AML detection. None of them can tell you how good it is — they know what they caught, and there is no way to know what they missed. LaunderLab builds the missing answer key: a synthetic bank, six real laundering typologies injected with transaction-level ground truth, a four-layer detection stack, an investigator workbench, and a red team that adapts to whatever gets caught.

A cyber range, but for financial crime

Security teams have had adversarial ranges for years: a red team attacks, a blue team defends, and you measure who wins. Financial-crime teams have nothing equivalent, because you cannot safely publish real laundering data — and without ground truth you cannot score a detector honestly. LaunderLab generates the data instead, and records the answer.

Research questions

Four questions, four measured answers

Three were the founding thesis. The fourth was forced by building the visual layer — and it changed how the other three should be read.

How fast does a detection stack rot against an adapting adversary?
Non-uniformly. One rule collapses to zero recall within two generations of adaptation and stays collapsed. Two others never fully evade across eight. A single aggregate recall figure could not have shown the difference.

Detection decay

What does a true alert actually cost to find?
Measured for every configuration, never estimated. The top 25 alerts are 100% true positives; the next 25 cost 10 false positives — 1.25 analyst reviews per true find at a budget of 50.

False-positive economics

What can a single institution structurally not see?
Banks flag 75–77% of individual mule accounts on their own books and reconstruct 0–6% of the chains those accounts form. Privacy-preserving co-operation recovers 69–81% of hops without sharing customer data.

The cross-bank blind spot

Was the alert soon enough to matter? (added by Phase 9)
“Caught” was never one property. The typology this stack detects fastest is caught with 100% of the money already moved, because the rule that catches it cannot fire until the crime has completed.

Why trust any of this

Seventeen times a flattering number turned out to be an artefact

Each one was measured, corrected, and written down rather than quietly kept. That record is the most valuable thing in the project.

A perfect ML score that was a data bug

Gradient boosting scored a flawless 1.000 average precision. The legitimate world emitted zero cash transactions, so the model had simply learned “cash means crime”.

100% rule precision that was a property of the world

Fixing the cash gap took one rule from 0 to 24 false positives on a clean world. The original precision had been an artefact of a bank where nobody legitimately banked cash.

Green CI that was running 178 of 226 tests

Three modules skipped on a missing dependency — including two of the checks that enforce the project's core boundary. A skipped module counts as one skip, so 48 missing tests hid behind the number 3.

A risk score whose top band was unreachable

Every case in the bank was low or medium and the highest score achievable was 43.5. “High” and “critical” described nothing — found when a SAR draft called a confirmed structuring scheme low risk.

The pattern behind almost all of them

They surfaced by rendering a number where a person had to read it next to a decision. Detection metrics grade a detector against ground truth. Nothing grades whether its output is usable — or whether it is being graded on the right axis at all.

And the limits are published too

The world is synthetic and its realism bounds every figure. The benchmarks use one seed. The co-operation protocol is a prototype whose residual disclosure is stated rather than glossed. Alert-to-SAR conversion is reported as not measurable rather than as zero, because no case in this world has been worked to a disposition.

The boundary that makes it meaningful

No detection code may read the ground-truth tables. If a detector could see the answer key, every precision and recall figure here would be meaningless — and it would fail silently. Enforced by source-level tests in twelve places.

Every page is generated, never typed

The figures on these pages are rendered from the same scoring modules the test suite grades. A hand-written page with results pasted in would look identical on day one and be silently wrong the first time a threshold moved.
